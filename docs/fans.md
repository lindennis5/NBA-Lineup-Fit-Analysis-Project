# What Does This Actually Mean for NBA Fans?

*Running example: the 2024–25 New York Knicks. The author is a Knicks fan, and the core below was chosen because it shared the most possessions, not because of how it performed.*

## Why teams weigh fit

Nobody builds a roster by collecting the five best box scores. A lineup of great shooters with nobody to rebound struggles on the glass. A lineup with no one to create a shot stalls late in the clock. So teams talk about "fit": the idea that the right fifth starter is the one who covers what the other four lack, not simply the most talented player available.

## How a four-player core has gaps

Take the Knicks' Karl-Anthony Towns, OG Anunoby, Josh Hart and Mikal Bridges. Compare each of their previous-season numbers with the league average in five areas: spacing, playmaking, creation, rebounding and blocks. Their average sits below the league in blocks, by about a third of a standard deviation. That is their largest gap. It is a small one, and that matters: most cores in this study have gaps that small, so "filling the biggest hole" often means filling a hole nobody would call big.

## How fifth players are compared

Many fifth players shared the floor with the same four, so we can compare them directly: same four teammates, different fifth player. For each one we measure how much of the core's biggest gap he fills, then ask whether lineups did better when that number was higher, after accounting for how good the fifth player was overall and what his skills were. Jalen Brunson shows the limits of the score. His block rate cannot fill the Knicks' gap, so he scores zero, despite elite creation and playmaking. Miles McBride, Cameron Payne and Delon Wright also score zero. A score that cannot separate those four is a blunt instrument.

## What the findings suggest

In four seasons of lineup data, filling the biggest gap did not beat simply playing the better fifth player. The estimate is essentially zero, with room for modest effects of either sign. Adding the score also did not improve predictions of the next season's lineups. The result is the same in the two seasons whose data passed an independent check as in all four.

## Why the null does not disprove chemistry

A null result here says one specific thing: this particular score, built from box-score proxies, adds nothing once talent and skills are counted. It leaves a lot standing. Blocks are not defense. Game Score is not impact. Passing gravity, switching, effort and trust do not show up in these numbers. Coaches also choose their lineups, so the lineups we can see are not a fair experiment. And five-player units are small samples, so real effects can hide in the noise. Chemistry may matter and be invisible to this method.

## What fans, coaches and front offices can conclude

- **Fans:** talent and playing time explain most of what you see. Be skeptical of claims that a player "fills a hole" unless someone shows evidence.
- **Coaches:** do not use this gap score to pick between fifth players. It cannot even distinguish the Knicks' four candidates.
- **Front offices:** do not pay for fit as measured here. The data cannot show fit pays off, but they also cannot rule out a modest benefit, so keep scouting judgment in the mix.

## What remains unanswered

Whether better measures (tracking data, defensive impact, shooting gravity) find a fit effect these box-score proxies cannot. Whether a different definition of fit does better: three alternatives were set out in advance, including one that credits skill wherever the core is weak, and all three came out null ([audit](FINAL_AUDIT.md)). Whether the two seasons with unresolved data issues change anything once the provider's counts are fixed. And whether any of this reflects cause and effect, which a study of observed lineups cannot say.
